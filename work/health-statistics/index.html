Interpreting supplied statistical outputs

This revised sample examines how demographic characteristics relate to BMI, cholesterol and reported heart-attack history. The supplied report contains descriptive statistics, a two-sample t-test, one-way ANOVA, chi-square tests and linear regression. It demonstrates statistical interpretation, technical reporting and communication.

Evidence boundaryThis sample is a revision and interpretation based on supplied statistical outputs. The original dataset and analytical script were not available for independent replication.

Source, sample and analytical scope

The source is the supplied Statistical Analysis Sample PDF, which transcribes Tables 1–14 from statistical_analysis_report.docx. The overall sample contains 1,116 observations; available cases vary across analyses. Original data provenance and collection dates are unspecified. BMI is measured in kg/m²; cholesterol and vitamin C units require verification against a codebook.

The female-minus-male mean difference is 0.365 BMI units (95% CI -0.198 to 0.928). The reported equal-variance test gives t(1,114) = 1.273, p = 0.203. This provides insufficient evidence of a difference in mean BMI; it does not prove the means are equal.

Reported standard deviations differ between groups. A Welch t-test would be a useful sensitivity check with the original data, but was not run for this revision.

Adjusted associations with cholesterol

The regression includes age, BMI, vitamin C and sex. Its degrees of freedom imply 1,077 complete cases: F(4, 1,072) = 67.43, p < 0.001; R² = 0.201. The model accounts for 20.1% of observed cholesterol variation within its estimation sample.

The ANOVA results indicate differences somewhere among the education and self-rated health groups; they do not identify which groups differ. Reported heart-attack history is 46/564 (8.16%) among male participants and 16/552 (2.90%) among female participants. This is an unadjusted sample comparison, not population prevalence. The rural/urban comparison does not provide statistically significant evidence of an association.

Coding, representation and replication

The sex variable’s numeric coding remains unresolved, so no direction is assigned to its regression coefficient. Sampling design, survey weights, definitions and missing-data handling are insufficiently documented to assess population representativeness. Cholesterol and vitamin C units are unconfirmed.

The model was not validated on held-out data. Original data and scripts are needed to reproduce results, examine influential observations and residuals, and assess standard-error choices. Findings are statistical associations; they are not causal effects, medical recommendations or population-level prevalence estimates.

Revision and available sample

The supplied document revises the presentation and interpretation of existing outputs, with AI-assisted language and presentation. It is not an independent rerun. The website transcribes those reported statistics and preserves their limitations.
